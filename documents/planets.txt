OVERVIEW A planet is a celestial body that orbits a star, is massive
enough for its gravity to make it nearly round, and has cleared most
other objects from its orbital neighborhood. In our Solar System, eight
planets orbit the Sun. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus,
Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.

The Solar System also contains dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, comets,
meteoroids, and dust. Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet, not one of
the eight major planets.

THE SUN AND PLANETARY ORBITS The Sun is the star at the center of the
Solar System. Its gravity keeps the planets in orbit. A planet’s
rotation is its spin around its own axis. A planet’s revolution, or
orbital period, is the time it takes to complete one orbit around the
Sun. A planet’s year is based on its orbital period. A planet’s day is
related to its rotation, although the exact definition can vary
depending on how rotation is measured.

THE EIGHT PLANETS

1.  MERCURY Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun and the smallest of
    the eight planets. It is a rocky, terrestrial planet with a heavily
    cratered surface. Mercury has almost no substantial atmosphere; it
    has a very thin exosphere. Because it has little atmosphere to
    retain heat, temperatures vary greatly between its day and night
    sides. Mercury has no natural moons and no rings. It takes about 88
    Earth days to orbit the Sun. Despite being closest to the Sun,
    Mercury is not the hottest planet; Venus is hotter because of its
    strong greenhouse effect.

2.  VENUS Venus is the second planet from the Sun. It is similar to
    Earth in size, but its environment is extremely different. Venus has
    a thick atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide and clouds
    containing sulfuric acid. Its powerful greenhouse effect makes it
    the hottest planet in the Solar System, with surface temperatures
    hot enough to melt lead. Venus rotates very slowly and in the
    opposite direction to most planets, a motion called retrograde
    rotation. It has no natural moons and no rings. Its orbit around the
    Sun takes about 225 Earth days.

3.  EARTH Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only world
    currently known to support life. Its surface has liquid water, and
    its atmosphere is rich in nitrogen and oxygen. Earth has a suitable
    range of temperatures for life as we know it, due to factors
    including its distance from the Sun, atmosphere, and magnetic field.
    About 71 percent of Earth’s surface is covered by water. Earth has
    one natural moon and no rings. One orbit around the Sun takes about
    365.25 days, which is why leap years help keep the calendar aligned
    with Earth’s orbit.

4.  MARS Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun. It is often called the
    Red Planet because iron minerals in its soil oxidize, or rust,
    giving the surface a reddish color. Mars is a cold, rocky desert
    world with a thin atmosphere composed mostly of carbon dioxide. It
    has polar ice caps, ancient river channels, volcanoes, and evidence
    that liquid water existed on its surface in the distant past.
    Scientists investigate Mars to understand its history and whether it
    could once have supported microbial life. Mars has two small moons,
    Phobos and Deimos. Its year is about 687 Earth days.

5.  JUPITER Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun and the largest
    planet in the Solar System. It is a gas giant composed mainly of
    hydrogen and helium and does not have a solid surface like Earth.
    Jupiter has colorful cloud bands and the Great Red Spot, a giant
    storm that has lasted for centuries. It has a powerful magnetic
    field and a large family of moons. Four famous large moons
    discovered by Galileo are Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto.
    Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System. Jupiter takes
    about 11.86 Earth years to orbit the Sun and has faint rings.

6.  SATURN Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the
    second-largest planet. It is a gas giant made mainly of hydrogen and
    helium. Saturn is famous for its extensive ring system, which
    consists mostly of pieces of water ice mixed with rocky material and
    dust. The rings are wide but very thin compared with their width.
    Saturn has many moons, including Titan, which has a thick
    atmosphere, and Enceladus, which releases water-rich plumes from its
    icy surface. Saturn takes about 29.5 Earth years to orbit the Sun.

7.  URANUS Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun. It is an ice
    giant, containing hydrogen and helium along with larger amounts of
    water, ammonia, and methane-related materials deep inside. Methane
    in its atmosphere absorbs red light and helps give Uranus its
    blue-green appearance. Uranus rotates on its side, with an extreme
    axial tilt of about 98 degrees. This produces unusual seasonal
    patterns. It has a faint ring system and many known moons. Uranus
    takes about 84 Earth years to orbit the Sun.

8.  NEPTUNE Neptune is the eighth and farthest recognized major planet
    from the Sun. It is an ice giant with an atmosphere mostly made of
    hydrogen and helium, with methane contributing to its blue
    appearance. Neptune experiences very fast winds and powerful storms.
    Its largest moon, Triton, travels around Neptune in a retrograde
    orbit, opposite the planet’s direction of rotation. Neptune has
    faint rings and takes about 165 Earth years to complete one orbit
    around the Sun.

TERRESTRIAL PLANETS AND GIANT PLANETS The four inner planets—Mercury,
Venus, Earth, and Mars—are called terrestrial planets. They are
relatively small, dense, and rocky, with solid surfaces.

Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants. They are very large and consist
mainly of hydrogen and helium.

Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. They contain greater proportions of
substances such as water, ammonia, and methane-related materials than
Jupiter and Saturn do. The term “ice” describes planetary science
categories and does not mean that the planets are simply frozen solid
balls.

DWARF PLANETS A dwarf planet orbits the Sun and is nearly round due to
its own gravity, but it has not cleared its orbital neighborhood and is
not a moon. Pluto is the best-known dwarf planet. Other recognized dwarf
planets include Ceres, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake. Dwarf planets are
different from the eight major planets because of their orbital
neighborhoods and classification.

MOONS, RINGS, AND ATMOSPHERES A natural satellite, commonly called a
moon, orbits a planet or another body. Some planets have no moons, while
others have many. Rings are collections of small particles orbiting a
planet. Saturn has the most visually prominent ring system, but Jupiter,
Uranus, and Neptune also have rings. Planetary atmospheres vary widely:
Earth has an atmosphere that supports life as we know it, Venus has a
very dense carbon-dioxide atmosphere, Mars has a thin atmosphere, and
the giant planets have deep gaseous atmospheres.

HOW SCIENTISTS STUDY PLANETS Scientists study planets using telescopes,
spacecraft flybys, orbiters, landers, and rovers. Telescopes observe
planets from Earth or space. Orbiters circle a planet to map its surface
and measure its atmosphere and magnetic field. Landers and rovers
examine a surface directly. Spacecraft have visited all eight planets,
although the amount and type of data available differ by planet.
Scientists combine observations and physical models to learn about
planetary composition, weather, geology, origins, and potential
habitability.

COMMON QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS

Q: How many planets are in the Solar System? A: There are eight
recognized major planets in the Solar System.

Q: What is the order of the planets from the Sun? A: Mercury, Venus,
Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

Q: Which planet is closest to the Sun? A: Mercury.

Q: Which planet is the hottest? A: Venus, because its thick
carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps heat through an intense greenhouse
effect.

Q: Which planet is the largest? A: Jupiter.

Q: Which planet is known as the Red Planet? A: Mars, because iron-rich
surface materials give it a reddish appearance.

Q: Which planet is famous for its rings? A: Saturn is famous for its
bright, extensive rings, although other giant planets also have rings.

Q: Which planet is farthest from the Sun among the eight planets? A:
Neptune.

Q: Which planet is known to support life? A: Earth is the only planet
currently known to support life.

Q: Is Pluto a planet? A: Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet under the
current standard astronomical classification.

Q: Do all planets have moons? A: No. Mercury and Venus have no natural
moons. Earth has one, Mars has two, and the giant planets have multiple
moons.

Q: What is the difference between rotation and revolution? A: Rotation
is a body’s spin around its axis. Revolution is its motion around
another body, such as a planet orbiting the Sun.

Q: Why is Venus hotter than Mercury? A: Venus has a dense atmosphere
that traps heat very effectively. Mercury has almost no atmosphere to
retain heat.

Q: What is the difference between a gas giant and an ice giant? A: Gas
giants Jupiter and Saturn are dominated by hydrogen and helium. Ice
giants Uranus and Neptune contain larger proportions of substances such
as water, ammonia, and methane-related materials.
