1.  WHAT IS A SPACE MISSION? A space mission is a planned operation that
    sends a spacecraft, satellite, crew, instrument, or robotic vehicle
    into space to achieve scientific, commercial, technological, or
    exploration goals. Missions may study Earth, the Sun, the Moon,
    planets, asteroids, comets, distant stars, galaxies, or the space
    environment.

Common mission types: - Earth observation: monitors weather, climate,
forests, oceans, agriculture, disasters, and land use. - Communication:
relays television, internet, telephone, and other data. - Navigation:
provides positioning, navigation, and timing services. - Astronomy:
studies stars, galaxies, black holes, exoplanets, and the early
universe. - Planetary exploration: investigates the Moon, Mars, Venus,
asteroids, comets, and outer planets. - Human spaceflight: carries
astronauts to spacecraft, laboratories, or other celestial
destinations. - Technology demonstration: tests new propulsion, docking,
navigation, robotics, or spacecraft systems. - Space weather: studies
solar activity and its effects on Earth and spacecraft.

2.  IMPORTANT SPACE AGENCIES AND ORGANIZATIONS NASA: The United States
    National Aeronautics and Space Administration. It conducts human
    spaceflight, Earth science, astronomy, planetary exploration, and
    aeronautics research. ISRO: The Indian Space Research Organisation.
    It develops Indian launch vehicles, satellites, navigation systems,
    Earth-observation missions, and science and human-spaceflight
    programs. ESA: The European Space Agency. It coordinates space
    activities among participating European countries and conducts
    science, Earth-observation, and exploration missions. Roscosmos:
    Russia’s state space corporation, involved in launch services, human
    spaceflight, and space infrastructure. CNSA: The China National
    Space Administration, responsible for China’s civil space
    exploration activities. JAXA: The Japan Aerospace Exploration
    Agency, involved in launch systems, astronomy, Earth observation,
    asteroid exploration, and human-spaceflight cooperation. CSA: The
    Canadian Space Agency, contributing to robotics, Earth observation,
    and international human-spaceflight projects. SpaceX: A private
    aerospace company that develops launch vehicles and spacecraft,
    including Falcon rockets, Dragon spacecraft, and Starship. Other
    important organizations include the United Arab Emirates Space
    Agency, South Korea’s space program, and commercial satellite and
    launch companies.

3.  EARLY SPACE AGE: 1957–1969 Sputnik 1 (Soviet Union, 1957): The first
    artificial satellite to orbit Earth. Its launch on October 4, 1957,
    is widely regarded as the beginning of the Space Age. Explorer 1
    (United States, 1958): The first successful U.S. satellite. Its
    instruments helped discover the Van Allen radiation belts. Luna 1
    (Soviet Union, 1959): One of the first spacecraft to reach the
    vicinity of the Moon and enter a heliocentric orbit. Luna 2 (Soviet
    Union, 1959): The first human-made object to reach the Moon’s
    surface. Luna 3 (Soviet Union, 1959): Returned the first photographs
    of the Moon’s far side. Vostok 1 (Soviet Union, 1961): Yuri Gagarin
    became the first human to travel into space and orbit Earth on April
    12, 1961. Mercury-Redstone 3 / Freedom 7 (United States, 1961): Alan
    Shepard became the first American in space on a suborbital flight.
    Mariner 2 (United States, 1962): The first successful planetary
    flyby, studying Venus. Vostok 6 (Soviet Union, 1963): Valentina
    Tereshkova became the first woman in space. Mariner 4 (United
    States, 1964 launch; Mars flyby in 1965): Returned the first
    close-up images of Mars. Luna 9 (Soviet Union, 1966): Made the first
    successful soft landing on the Moon and transmitted images from the
    surface. Apollo 8 (United States, 1968): The first crewed mission to
    orbit the Moon. Apollo 11 (United States, 1969): Neil Armstrong and
    Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the Moon on July 20,
    1969; Michael Collins remained in lunar orbit.

4.  SPACE STATIONS AND REUSABLE SPACECRAFT Salyut 1 (Soviet Union,
    1971): The first space station placed in orbit. Skylab (United
    States, 1973): The first U.S. space station, used for research in
    microgravity and observations of the Sun and Earth. Apollo-Soyuz
    Test Project (1975): A joint U.S.-Soviet mission that demonstrated
    cooperation and spacecraft docking. Mir (Soviet Union/Russia,
    1986–2001): A modular space station that supported long-duration
    human spaceflight and international research. Space Shuttle (United
    States, 1981–2011): A reusable crewed spacecraft system used to
    deploy satellites, conduct experiments, and help build the
    International Space Station. International Space Station (ISS): An
    international orbital laboratory continuously occupied since
    November 2000. It supports research in biology, physics, human
    health, materials science, and technology.

5.  MAJOR PLANETARY EXPLORATION MISSIONS Pioneer 10 (United States,
    launched 1972): The first spacecraft to pass through the asteroid
    belt and make a close encounter with Jupiter. Pioneer 11 (United
    States, launched 1973): Explored Jupiter and became the first
    spacecraft to fly past Saturn. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 (United
    States, launched 1977): Explored the outer planets. Voyager 1 and
    Voyager 2 later entered interstellar space, becoming the first two
    spacecraft to do so. They continue to send limited scientific data
    when power and communication permit; their status can change. Viking
    1 and Viking 2 (United States, 1975 launches): Landers and orbiters
    that studied Mars. Viking 1 achieved the first fully successful U.S.
    Mars landing in 1976. Galileo (United States, launched 1989):
    Studied Jupiter and its moons; entered Jupiter orbit in 1995.
    Cassini-Huygens (NASA, ESA, and Italian Space Agency, launched
    1997): Studied Saturn, its rings, and moons. The Huygens probe
    landed on Titan in 2005. Cassini’s mission ended in 2017. Mars
    Pathfinder (United States, 1996 launch): Delivered the Sojourner
    rover to Mars in 1997, demonstrating a lower-cost approach to
    robotic exploration. Mars Exploration Rovers, Spirit and Opportunity
    (United States, 2003 launches): Investigated Martian geology and
    evidence of ancient water. Opportunity operated far longer than
    originally planned. MESSENGER (United States, launched 2004):
    Orbited Mercury and greatly improved knowledge of its surface and
    magnetic field. Mission ended in 2015. New Horizons (United States,
    launched 2006): Flew past Pluto in 2015, providing the first
    close-up views of Pluto and its moons, then explored Kuiper Belt
    object Arrokoth in 2019. Juno (United States, launched 2011):
    Studies Jupiter’s gravity, magnetic field, atmosphere, and interior.
    Dawn (United States, launched 2007): Orbited Vesta and Ceres, two
    major bodies in the asteroid belt. Mission ended in 2018. OSIRIS-REx
    (United States, launched 2016): Collected samples from asteroid
    Bennu and delivered them to Earth in 2023. The spacecraft continued
    under the OSIRIS-APEX mission designation toward asteroid Apophis.
    Parker Solar Probe (United States, launched 2018): Flies through the
    Sun’s outer atmosphere, the corona, to study solar wind and solar
    processes. BepiColombo (ESA/JAXA, launched 2018): A joint mission to
    study Mercury. Consult official mission updates for its current
    cruise and arrival schedule. Europa Clipper (United States, launched
    2024): Travels to Jupiter to investigate Europa’s ice-covered ocean
    world and assess its habitability. It is not designed to land on
    Europa. JUICE (ESA, launched 2023): Travels to Jupiter to study the
    planet and icy moons, especially Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa.
    Dragonfly (NASA, planned rotorcraft mission): Designed to explore
    Saturn’s moon Titan. Its launch and arrival dates should be checked
    against current official updates.

6.  MOON EXPLORATION Luna missions: The Soviet Luna program achieved
    major early milestones, including the first impact on the Moon, the
    first far-side images, and early soft landings. Ranger missions:
    U.S. missions that returned close-up images of the lunar surface
    before impact. Surveyor missions: U.S. robotic landers that tested
    lunar soil and landing conditions ahead of Apollo. Apollo 11, 12,
    14, 15, 16, and 17: Crewed Apollo missions that landed astronauts on
    the Moon. Apollo 13 did not land because an onboard failure forced
    the crew to abort the landing and return safely. Clementine (United
    States, 1994): Mapped the Moon and helped identify areas of interest
    near the poles. Lunar Prospector (United States, 1998): Studied
    lunar composition and provided evidence related to hydrogen near the
    lunar poles. SMART-1 (ESA, 2003): Tested solar-electric propulsion
    and studied the Moon. Chang’e 1 and Chang’e 2 (China): Orbital
    missions that mapped and studied the Moon. Chang’e 3 (China, 2013):
    Landed on the Moon and deployed the Yutu rover. Chang’e 4 (China,
    2019): Achieved the first soft landing on the lunar far side and
    deployed the Yutu-2 rover. Chang’e 5 (China, 2020): Returned lunar
    samples to Earth. Chandrayaan-1 (India, 2008): Orbited the Moon and
    contributed evidence for water-related molecules on the lunar
    surface. Chandrayaan-2 (India, 2019): Its orbiter continued lunar
    science after the lander failed during descent. Chandrayaan-3
    (India, 2023): Achieved a successful soft landing near the lunar
    south polar region and deployed the Pragyan rover. India became the
    fourth country to achieve a soft lunar landing. Luna 25 (Russia,
    2023): Attempted a lunar landing but crashed before landing. SLIM
    (Japan, 2024): Demonstrated precision lunar landing technology.
    Odysseus (Intuitive Machines, United States, 2024): Achieved a lunar
    landing as part of a commercial lunar delivery mission, though the
    lander ended up tilted. Blue Ghost (Firefly Aerospace, United
    States, 2025): A commercial robotic lunar lander that successfully
    delivered science and technology payloads to the Moon. Artemis
    program (NASA and partners): A series of missions intended to return
    astronauts to the lunar environment, develop capabilities for
    sustained exploration, and prepare for future crewed missions to
    Mars. Check NASA for the latest mission dates and status.

7.  MARS EXPLORATION Mariner 4: First successful Mars flyby and early
    close-up images. Viking 1 and Viking 2: Orbiters and landers that
    conducted imaging, weather observations, and biological experiments.
    Mars Global Surveyor (United States, 1996 launch): Mapped Mars and
    studied its surface, atmosphere, and magnetic properties. Mars
    Odyssey (United States, 2001 launch): Maps the surface and studies
    minerals and hydrogen distribution; its operational status should be
    checked with NASA. Mars Express (ESA, 2003 launch): Studies Mars’
    surface, subsurface, atmosphere, and water history. Spirit and
    Opportunity: Rovers that found evidence of ancient environments
    shaped by water. Curiosity (NASA, landed 2012): A rover studying
    Gale Crater, geology, climate, and past habitability. Mangalyaan /
    Mars Orbiter Mission (India, launched 2013): India’s first Mars
    mission and a successful Mars orbiter; it is no longer operational.
    MAVEN (NASA, launched 2013): Studies how Mars’ atmosphere interacts
    with the solar wind and how atmospheric gases escape to space.
    ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter (ESA/Roscosmos cooperation, launched
    2016): Studies trace gases and maps the Martian atmosphere. Consult
    ESA for current operational status. Hope / Emirates Mars Mission
    (UAE, launched 2020): Studies the Martian atmosphere and weather.
    Tianwen-1 (China, launched 2020): Included an orbiter, lander, and
    rover; the Zhurong rover landed on Mars in 2021. Perseverance (NASA,
    landed 2021): Studies geology and ancient habitability in Jezero
    Crater and collects samples for possible future return to Earth.
    Ingenuity (NASA): The small helicopter carried by Perseverance
    completed the first powered, controlled flights on another planet.
    Its flight operations ended in 2024 after rotor damage. Mars Sample
    Return: A proposed international effort to bring samples collected
    by Perseverance to Earth. Architecture, schedule, and funding have
    been under review; do not present a specific future launch date as
    certain without checking current official sources.

8.  INDIA’S SPACE PROGRAM: ISRO Aryabhata (1975): India’s first
    satellite, launched with Soviet assistance. SLV-3 and Rohini (1980):
    The SLV-3 successfully placed the Rohini satellite into orbit, a
    major milestone in India’s launch-vehicle development. INSAT and
    GSAT programs: Support communications, broadcasting, meteorology,
    and related services. IRS and Cartosat programs: Support remote
    sensing, mapping, agriculture, water resources, urban planning, and
    disaster management. NavIC: India’s regional satellite navigation
    system, designed to provide positioning and timing services.
    Chandrayaan-1 (2008): India’s first lunar mission; studied the Moon
    and contributed to discoveries related to lunar water. Mars Orbiter
    Mission / Mangalyaan (2013): Made India the first country to reach
    Mars orbit on its first attempt. The mission is no longer
    operational. AstroSat (2015): India’s multi-wavelength space
    observatory. Chandrayaan-2 (2019): The orbiter continues to provide
    lunar observations according to ISRO mission records; the lander did
    not achieve a soft landing. Chandrayaan-3 (2023): Successfully
    landed Vikram and deployed Pragyan near the lunar south polar
    region. The surface mission was short-lived, but its landing was a
    major milestone. Aditya-L1 (launched 2023): India’s solar
    observatory placed in a halo orbit around the Sun-Earth L1 region.
    It studies the Sun’s atmosphere, solar wind, and space weather.
    XPoSat (launched 2024): India’s X-ray Polarimeter Satellite,
    designed to study the polarization of X-ray emissions from
    astronomical sources. SpaDeX (launched December 2024): Demonstrated
    autonomous docking and undocking between two small spacecraft,
    including power transfer. This is an important technology for future
    servicing, station, and crewed missions. NISAR (launched July 30,
    2025): Joint NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar mission using L-band
    and S-band radar to observe changes in Earth’s land and ice
    surfaces, ecosystems, and hazards. Axiom Mission 4 (2025): Indian
    astronaut Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla flew to the International
    Space Station and conducted microgravity experiments, becoming the
    first Indian to visit the ISS. NVS-02 (2025): Navigation satellite
    launched by ISRO; ISRO records list it as not operational. Avoid
    describing it as fully operational. EOS-09 / PSLV-C61 (May 2025):
    Mission not accomplished because of a launch-vehicle issue. CMS-03
    (November 2025): Communication satellite launched by LVM3-M5.
    BlueBird Block 2 (December 2025): Commercial communications
    satellite launched by LVM3-M6; ISRO described it as the heaviest
    satellite launched from Indian soil at that time. PSLV-C62 / EOS-N1
    (January 2026): ISRO reported that the mission encountered an
    anomaly during the third stage and was not accomplished. GSLV-F17 /
    EOS-05 (September 4, 2026): ISRO reports that the mission
    successfully placed EOS-05 into its intended orbit. Gaganyaan:
    India’s human-spaceflight program, intended to demonstrate crewed
    orbital flight and related technologies. Test flights, crewed launch
    dates, and mission plans can change; consult ISRO’s latest official
    updates before giving a date as confirmed.

9.  OTHER MAJOR SPACE SCIENCE MISSIONS Hubble Space Telescope (NASA/ESA,
    launched 1990): Observes the universe across ultraviolet, visible,
    and near-infrared wavelengths and has contributed to discoveries in
    cosmology and galaxy evolution. Chandra X-ray Observatory (NASA,
    launched 1999): Observes high-energy X-rays from objects such as
    black holes, supernova remnants, and galaxy clusters. Kepler (NASA,
    launched 2009): Discovered thousands of exoplanet candidates and
    confirmed planets, transforming exoplanet research. Mission ended
    in 2018. TESS (NASA, launched 2018): Searches for exoplanets around
    nearby bright stars. James Webb Space Telescope / JWST (NASA, ESA,
    CSA, launched 2021): Observes infrared light to study early
    galaxies, star formation, exoplanets, and planetary atmospheres.
    Euclid (ESA, launched 2023): Maps galaxies and cosmic structure to
    investigate dark matter and dark energy. XRISM (JAXA/NASA
    collaboration, launched 2023): Studies X-rays from energetic cosmic
    objects and hot gas. XPoSat (India, launched 2024): Studies X-ray
    polarization from bright astronomical sources. SPHEREx (NASA,
    launched 2025): A space telescope designed to survey the sky in
    near-infrared wavelengths and study cosmic history, galaxies, and
    the ingredients for life in the Milky Way. Check current NASA
    records for mission status and released results. Nancy Grace Roman
    Space Telescope (NASA): A planned space observatory for dark-energy
    research, exoplanets, and wide-field infrared surveys. Verify launch
    status and dates from NASA.

10. EARTH OBSERVATION, WEATHER, AND NAVIGATION Earth-observation
    satellites monitor the planet’s surface, atmosphere, oceans, ice,
    vegetation, and disasters. Their data support weather forecasts,
    climate science, crop monitoring, flood mapping, wildfire detection,
    and urban planning. Weather satellites observe clouds, storms, water
    vapor, and atmospheric conditions. Communication satellites relay
    voice, video, and data over long distances. Navigation satellites
    provide timing and positioning signals. Examples include GPS (United
    States), Galileo (European Union), GLONASS (Russia), BeiDou (China),
    and NavIC (India). Radar satellites can observe the surface through
    clouds and at night. Optical satellites generally rely on reflected
    sunlight and can be affected by clouds. NISAR is an example of a
    radar mission designed to measure changes in Earth’s surface and
    ecosystems.

11. COMMERCIAL SPACEFLIGHT Commercial companies launch satellites,
    deliver cargo and crew to orbit, provide broadband internet, and
    develop lunar and deep-space technologies. Falcon 9: SpaceX’s
    partially reusable orbital launch vehicle. Dragon: SpaceX spacecraft
    used for cargo and crew transport. Starlink: A large satellite
    constellation designed to provide broadband internet; constellation
    size and service availability change over time. New Glenn: Blue
    Origin’s heavy-lift orbital launch vehicle. Consult official records
    for mission-specific status. Commercial lunar landers: Companies
    such as Intuitive Machines and Firefly Aerospace have delivered
    payloads to the lunar surface under commercial and government
    programs. Commercial missions can be successful, partially
    successful, or unsuccessful. The presence of a company or spacecraft
    name does not imply that every mission has succeeded.

12. IMPORTANT SPACE MISSION TERMS Launch vehicle: A rocket used to carry
    spacecraft or payloads into space. Payload: The satellite,
    instrument, crew, or cargo carried by a launch vehicle. Orbit: The
    curved path of one object around another because of gravity and
    motion. Low Earth orbit (LEO): An orbit relatively close to Earth,
    commonly used by crewed spacecraft and many Earth-observation
    satellites. Geostationary orbit (GEO): An equatorial orbit with a
    period matching Earth’s rotation, allowing a satellite to appear
    nearly fixed above one point on Earth. Lunar lander: A spacecraft
    designed to descend and operate on the Moon. Rover: A mobile robotic
    vehicle that explores a planetary surface. Flyby: A spacecraft
    passes near a celestial body without entering orbit around it.
    Orbiter: A spacecraft that travels around a celestial body. Sample
    return: A mission that collects material and returns it to Earth.
    Docking: Joining two spacecraft in orbit. Re-entry: The return of a
    spacecraft or object into an atmosphere from space. Telemetry: Data
    sent from a spacecraft to mission controllers. Deep space: Space
    beyond Earth’s immediate orbital environment; exact usage depends on
    context. Microgravity: Conditions in which objects experience
    apparent weightlessness while still subject to gravity. Solar wind:
    A stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun.
    Exoplanet: A planet orbiting a star other than the Sun.

13. WHY SPACE MISSIONS MATTER

-   Improve scientific understanding of Earth, the Solar System, and the
    universe.
-   Support weather forecasting, climate monitoring, agriculture,
    navigation, and communication.
-   Help detect and manage natural disasters.
-   Test technologies such as robotics, autonomous navigation,
    propulsion, docking, and life-support systems.
-   Investigate whether environments beyond Earth could have supported
    life.
-   Inspire education, engineering, international cooperation, and
    commercial innovation.
-   Provide data for planetary defense and the study of asteroids and
    comets.

14. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS Q: What was the first artificial
    satellite? A: Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union on October 4,
    1957.

Q: Who was the first human in space? A: Yuri Gagarin, who flew aboard
Vostok 1 on April 12, 1961.

Q: Who were the first humans to walk on the Moon? A: Neil Armstrong and
Buzz Aldrin during Apollo 11 in July 1969.

Q: Which country first landed a spacecraft softly on the Moon? A: The
Soviet Union’s Luna 9 achieved the first successful soft lunar landing
in 1966.

Q: Which country achieved a lunar soft landing in 2023? A: India,
through Chandrayaan-3. India became the fourth country to achieve a soft
lunar landing.

Q: What was India’s first satellite? A: Aryabhata, launched in 1975.

Q: What was India’s first Mars mission? A: The Mars Orbiter Mission,
also known as Mangalyaan, launched in 2013.

Q: What is Aditya-L1? A: An Indian solar observatory that studies the
Sun from the Sun-Earth L1 region.

Q: What is NISAR? A: A joint NASA-ISRO radar mission launched in 2025 to
measure changes in Earth’s land and ice, ecosystems, and related
processes.

Q: What is the ISS? A: The International Space Station, an international
orbital laboratory used for research and human spaceflight.

Q: What is the difference between a flyby and an orbiter? A: A flyby
passes near a target; an orbiter enters a bound orbit around it.

Q: What is a sample-return mission? A: A mission that collects material
from a target and returns it to Earth for laboratory analysis.

Q: Are all announced missions guaranteed to launch? A: No. Schedules can
change because of technical, budgetary, safety, and operational reasons.

15. GUIDANCE FOR AN LLM USING THIS FILE

-   Answer with clear mission names, countries or agencies, launch
    years, objectives, and achievements when known.
-   Distinguish a launch date from an arrival, landing, docking, or
    mission-completion date.
-   Distinguish successful, partially successful, failed, inactive, and
    planned missions.
-   Do not say that a mission is currently active unless a recent
    reliable source supports that claim.
-   Do not invent launch dates, crew names, discoveries, payloads, or
    mission outcomes.
-   If asked about the latest mission news, current spacecraft status,
    current astronaut assignments, or future schedules, verify official
    agency sources first.
-   Prefer primary sources such as NASA.gov, ISRO.gov.in, ESA.int,
    JAXA.jp, CNSA announcements, and Roscosmos publications.
-   Explain acronyms on first use.
-   If sources disagree, describe the disagreement and prefer recent
    official mission records.
-   This is a selected overview, not an exhaustive list of every
    national, military, commercial, or scientific mission.
